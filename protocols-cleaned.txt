LATARJET WITH REMPLISSAGE PROTOCOL



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Follow the Latarjet Protocol above with the following additional considerations:



The remplissage (infraspinatus tenodesis into the Hill-Sachs defect) does not significantly alter the rehabilitation timeline compared to isolated Latarjet.



Weeks 0-2: Period of Protection



Sling: Wear at all times except hygiene.



Pendulums permitted.



Begin gentle PROM: FF to 90°; ER to 20-30° at side.



No active biceps contraction against resistance.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase I):



Continue sling at all times.



Progress PROM: FF to 120°; ER to 30-40° at side.



No ABD with ER.



No active elbow flexion against resistance until 8 weeks.



Weeks 6-10 (Phase II):



Discontinue sling.



Begin AAROM, progress to AROM.



ER may be slightly limited compared to isolated Latarjet — progress per patient tolerance.



Weeks 10-16 (Phase III):



Progress to full AROM.



Isometrics at 10 weeks.



Resistance at 12 weeks.



Weeks 16+ (Phase IV):



Full strengthening.



Sport-specific at 4-5 months.



Contact sports at 6-9 months.



MMI at 9-12 months.



Note: Some mild loss of ER (5-10°) may persist due to the remplissage component. This is generally well tolerated and does not affect return to sport.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Tansey et al., Am J Sports Med, 2025 (Bankart + remplissage minimum 5-year outcomes — no significant ROM restriction); Davis et al., Am J Sports Med, 2023 (remplissage + Bankart vs. alternatives meta-analysis).

PROXIMAL HUMERUS ORIF PROTOCOL (4-PART FRACTURES)



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



4-part fractures have higher risk of AVN and fixation failure. Rehabilitation is more conservative than 2/3-part fractures.



Weeks 0-2: Period of Protection



Sling at all times.



Elbow/wrist/hand ROM.



Pendulums only.



No active shoulder motion.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase I):



Sling at all times between therapy sessions.



PROM only: FF to 90°, ER to 20-30°, ABD to 60°.



Progress PROM slowly — do not force ROM.



Weeks 6-10 (Phase II):



Discontinue sling at 6 weeks.



Begin AAROM cautiously.



Progress from supine to upright positions.



Weeks 10-16 (Phase III):



Progress to AROM.



Isometrics at 10-12 weeks.



Begin gentle resistance at 14-16 weeks.



Weeks 16+ (Phase IV):



Progressive strengthening with bands and light weights (1-3 lbs).



Full strengthening by 6 months.



Return to activity at 6+ months per radiographic healing.



Note: Monitor radiographs closely for AVN and loss of fixation. If AVN develops, strengthening may need to be delayed or modified. Tuberosity healing must be confirmed before advancing to resistance exercises.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Ma et al., Sci Rep, 2025 (locking plate fixation in 3- and 4-part PHF — complications and rehabilitation); Gavaskar et al., Clin Orthop Relat Res, 2022 (factors associated with poor outcomes after 3/4-part fracture-dislocation ORIF).

PROXIMAL HUMERUS INTRAMEDULLARY NAIL PROTOCOL (4-PART FRACTURES)



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Evidence shows comparable functional outcomes and rehabilitation timelines between IM nail and locking plate fixation for 3- and 4-part proximal humerus fractures. This protocol mirrors the 4-part ORIF protocol.



Weeks 0-2: Period of Protection



Sling at all times.



Elbow/wrist/hand ROM.



Pendulums only.



No active shoulder motion.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase I):



Sling at all times between therapy sessions.



PROM only: FF to 90°, ER to 20-30°, ABD to 60°.



Progress PROM slowly.



Weeks 6-10 (Phase II):



Discontinue sling at 6 weeks.



Begin AAROM cautiously.



Progress from supine to upright positions.



Weeks 10-16 (Phase III):



Progress to AROM.



Isometrics at 10-12 weeks.



Begin gentle resistance at 14-16 weeks.



Weeks 16+ (Phase IV):



Progressive strengthening with bands and light weights (1-3 lbs).



Full strengthening by 6 months.



Return to activity at 6+ months per radiographic healing.



Note: IM nail patients may experience rotator cuff irritation at the nail entry site. If supraspinatus symptoms develop, modify overhead strengthening accordingly. Hardware removal may be considered after union if symptomatic.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Vaccalluzzo et al., J Clin Med, 2025 (IM nail vs. locking plate — comparable outcomes); Boyer et al., Int Orthop, 2021 (RCT comparing IM nail vs. plate for 3/4-part PHF); Wikerøy et al., JBJS Open Access, 2025 (RCT — 2-year results, nail vs. plate).

LOWER TRAPEZIUS TENDON TRANSFER PROTOCOL



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-6: Period of Protection



Sling with abduction pillow at all times for 6 weeks.



No active shoulder motion.



Elbow/wrist/hand ONLY.



Pendulums.



Weeks 6-12 (Phase I):



Discontinue sling.



Begin PROM, progress to AAROM.



Scapular activation exercises under therapist guidance.



Tendon re-education: patient practices scapular retraction and depression to initiate ER.



Weeks 12-20 (Phase II):



AROM.



Isometrics.



Begin gentle resistance.



Focus on ER strengthening — the primary goal of the transfer.



Weeks 20+ (Phase III):



Progressive strengthening.



Full return at 9-12 months.



Note: Mean return to work is approximately 5 months; return to sport approximately 6 months. Retear of the transfer is the primary risk factor for failure to return to activity.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Kim et al., Am J Sports Med, 2025 (return to work/sport after LTT — 110 patients); Chopra et al., J Shoulder Elbow Surg, 2024 (outcomes after arthroscopically assisted LTT); Clouette et al., J Shoulder Elbow Surg, 2020 (systematic review of LTT).

MULTIDIRECTIONAL INSTABILITY (MDI) REPAIR PROTOCOL



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-2: Period of Protection



Sling: Wear at all times except hygiene.



No shoulder ROM for first 2 weeks.



Elbow/forearm/wrist/hand motion ONLY.



Pendulums, grip strengthening.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase I):



Continue sling at all times.



Begin gentle PROM at 2 weeks.



ROM limited in ALL directions — protect capsular plication.



FF to 90°; ER to 20-30°; controlled ABD to 60°.



No IR past neutral.



Weeks 6-10 (Phase II):



Discontinue sling at 6 weeks.



Continue PROM, begin AAROM.



FF to 120°; ER to 30-40°; controlled ABD.



Weeks 10-14 (Phase III):



Progress to AROM.



Begin isometrics.



Emphasis on scapular stabilization — critical in MDI patients.



Weeks 14-20 (Phase IV):



Begin resistance exercises.



Progressive strengthening.



Dynamic rotator cuff strengthening.



Proprioception and perturbation training.



Weeks 20+ (Phase V):



Sport-specific training.



Return to sports at 6-9 months.



Note: MDI patients have generalized capsular laxity. Rehabilitation must emphasize dynamic stabilizers (rotator cuff and scapular muscles) and proprioception more than in traumatic instability repairs. Avoid aggressive stretching — the goal is NOT to maximize ROM but to achieve functional ROM with stability.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Hippensteel et al., JAAOS, 2023 (comprehensive review of MDI); Gruskay et al., Am J Sports Med, 2022 (arthroscopic pancapsular shift — 9-year outcomes); Watson et al., J Shoulder Elbow Surg, 2018 (conservative rehab program for MDI).

FLEXOR TENDON REPAIR PROTOCOL — DIRECT PRIMARY REPAIR



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-4 (Phase I): Early Protected Motion



Dorsal blocking splint: Wrist 20-30° flexion, MCP 60-70° flexion, IP joints extended.



Begin early active motion protocol (preferred over passive-only protocols):



Active flexion through partial range (half-fist) for first 2-3 weeks.



40-80 cycles of active flexion per exercise session, 4-6 sessions per day.



Active extension to splint limits.



Place-and-hold exercises starting at 3-4 weeks (gentle active hold in flexion).



Edema management.



Scar massage once incisions closed.



Weeks 4-6 (Phase II): Active Motion



Continue dorsal blocking splint between exercises.



Progress to full-range active flexion/extension within protective range.



Tendon gliding exercises: straight fist, hook fist, full fist.



Blocking exercises for FDP and FDS.



Weeks 6-8 (Phase III): Progressive Motion



Discontinue splint (unless surgeon specifies otherwise).



Full AROM.



Composite fist.



Light functional activities.



Begin gentle resistance at 8 weeks.



Weeks 8-12 (Phase IV): Strengthening



Progressive grip strengthening with putty and hand exerciser.



Functional activities with increasing resistance.



Weeks 12+ (Phase V): Return to Activity



Full strengthening.



Return to sports/heavy labor at 3-4 months.



Maximum grip strength recovery at 4-6 months.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Tang JB, J Hand Surg Eur, 2021 (early passive-active flexion protocol); Trumble et al., JBJS Am, 2010 (RCT — active place-and-hold vs. passive motion); Tang et al., IFSSH Consensus, J Hand Surg Eur, 2026 (updated guidelines emphasizing true early active flexion).

FLEXOR TENDON RECONSTRUCTION — STAGE 1 (HUNTER ROD IMPLANTATION) PROTOCOL



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Stage 1 involves placement of a silicone rod to create a pseudosheath, with or without pulley reconstruction. The rod is NOT a functioning tendon — no active flexion is expected.



Weeks 0-3 (Phase I): Wound Healing and Protection



Dorsal blocking splint: Wrist 20-30° flexion, MCP 50-60° flexion, IP joints extended.



Wound care.



Passive flexion/extension of involved finger(s) within splint to maintain joint mobility and promote pseudosheath formation around the rod.



Active ROM of uninvolved fingers.



Edema management.



Weeks 3-6 (Phase II): Joint Mobilization



Continue dorsal blocking splint.



Passive ROM of all finger joints — critical to maintain supple joints for Stage 2.



If pulleys were reconstructed: passive motion only within protected range per surgeon.



Begin scar massage once incisions healed.



Weeks 6-12 (Phase III): Full Passive Motion



Transition to removable splint or buddy taping.



Full PROM of all finger joints — goal is supple, mobile joints.



Active extension exercises.



Blocking exercises (passive).



Intrinsic stretching.



Months 3-6 (Pre-Stage 2):



Continue daily PROM to maintain full passive joint motion.



Splinting for any developing contractures (static progressive or dynamic).



Stage 2 surgery typically performed at 3-6 months after Stage 1, once:



- Soft tissues are supple



- Full passive ROM is achieved



- Pseudosheath has matured



- Wounds are well healed



Note: The primary goal of Stage 1 rehabilitation is to achieve and maintain FULL PASSIVE ROM of all finger joints. If passive ROM is not achieved before Stage 2, outcomes will be compromised.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Samora JB, Klinefelter RD. Flexor tendon reconstruction. JAAOS, 2016; Sun et al., Orthopedics, 2010 (2-stage reconstruction using Hunter technique — 84% good/excellent results); Al-Qattan MM, Ann Plast Surg, 2019 (active ROM after Stage 1 silicone rod insertion).

FLEXOR TENDON RECONSTRUCTION — STAGE 2 (TENDON GRAFTING) PROTOCOL



========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Stage 2 involves removal of the silicone rod and placement of a tendon graft (palmaris longus, plantaris, or other) through the pseudosheath. The graft is secured distally to the distal phalanx and proximally via Pulvertaft weave or similar technique.



Weeks 0-4 (Phase I): Early Protected Motion



Dorsal blocking splint: Wrist 20-30° flexion, MCP 60-70° flexion, IP joints extended.



Early controlled motion protocol — similar to primary flexor tendon repair:



Passive flexion within the splint.



Active extension to splint limits.



Place-and-hold exercises starting at 3-4 weeks.



Edema management.



Scar massage once incisions closed.



Note: Tendon graft junctions (especially the proximal weave) may be weaker than a primary repair. Some surgeons prefer a more conservative (passive-only) protocol for the first 4 weeks. Follow surgeon preference.



Weeks 4-6 (Phase II): Active Motion



Continue dorsal blocking splint between exercises.



Begin gentle active flexion/extension within protective range.



Tendon gliding exercises.



Blocking exercises for individual joints.



Weeks 6-8 (Phase III): Progressive Motion



Discontinue splint (unless surgeon specifies otherwise).



Full AROM.



Composite fist.



Light functional activities.



Begin gentle resistance at 8 weeks.



Weeks 8-12 (Phase IV): Strengthening



Progressive grip strengthening with putty and hand exerciser.



Functional activities with increasing resistance.



Weeks 12+ (Phase V): Return to Activity



Full strengthening.



Return to heavy activities at 4-6 months.



Maximum grip strength recovery at 6-9 months.



Note: Outcomes after Stage 2 reconstruction are generally good (70-85% good/excellent results) but may not match primary repair outcomes. Tenolysis may be required at 6+ months if adhesions limit tendon glide despite compliant therapy.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Sun et al., Orthopedics, 2010 (2-stage reconstruction — 84% good/excellent); Samora JB, Klinefelter RD. JAAOS, 2016; Tang et al., IFSSH Consensus, J Hand Surg Eur, 2026 (early active motion can be used after one-stage tendon grafting — principles applicable to Stage 2).

DISTAL BICEPS TENDON REPAIR PROTOCOL



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-2: Period of Protection



Posterior splint at 90° flexion, forearm in neutral to slight supination.



NO active elbow flexion or forearm supination.



Finger/wrist/shoulder ROM permitted.



Note: For cortical button fixation, surgeon may allow earlier active ROM as tolerated per recent evidence supporting early mobilization with this technique.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase I):



Hinged elbow brace: 30-120° initially, progress 10° extension/week.



PROM and AAROM elbow flexion/extension.



No active supination against resistance.



No lifting.



Weeks 6-10 (Phase II):



Discontinue brace.



Full AROM.



Begin gentle active supination.



Isometric biceps at 8 weeks.



Weeks 10-16 (Phase III):



Progressive resistance: bands → weights (1-5 lbs).



Begin eccentric biceps exercises.



Weeks 16+ (Phase IV):



Full strengthening.



Return to heavy lifting at 5-6 months.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Simpson et al., Am J Sports Med, 2025 (systematic review/meta-analysis of early vs. delayed mobilization); Bergman et al., JBJS, 2021 (RCT of early mobilization with cortical button).

INTERVAL THROWING PROGRAM — PHASE I (FLAT GROUND)



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: Every other day



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Prerequisites: Full pain-free ROM, 90%+ strength compared to uninjured arm.



Throw every other day. Stop if pain occurs. Ice after each session.



Step 1 (Sessions 1-3): 45 feet, 25 throws at 50% effort. Rest 5 minutes. Repeat.

Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Days 0-3:



Dorsal blocking splint: PIP in 30° flexion.



Elevate hand.



Active DIP and MCP ROM.



Days 3-14 (Phase I): Early Protected Motion



Begin active PIP flexion within dorsal block splint.



Extension block: do not extend PIP past 30° initially.



Progress extension block by 10° per week.



Active DIP flexion/extension.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase II): Progressive Motion



Progress extension block to full extension by 4-6 weeks.



Full AROM.



Buddy taping for support.



Tendon gliding exercises.



Weeks 6-12 (Phase III): Strengthening



Discontinue splint.



Progressive grip and pinch strengthening.



PROM for residual stiffness.



Functional activities.



Weeks 12+:



Full activity.



Return to sport at 3-4 months.



Note: Hemi-hamate autograft restores the volar lip of the middle phalanx. Early motion is critical to prevent PIP stiffness. The dorsal block protocol is similar to PIP fracture-dislocation ORIF but may be advanced slightly faster if graft fixation is rigid.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Williams RMM, Kiefhaber TR, Sommerkamp TG, Stern PJ. Treatment of unstable dorsal proximal interphalangeal fracture/dislocations using a hemi-hamate autograft. J Hand Surg Am, 2003; Calfee RP, Kiefhaber TR, Sommerkamp TG, Stern PJ. Hemi-hamate arthroplasty provides functional reconstruction of acute and chronic PIP fracture-dislocations. J Hand Surg Am, 2009.

EXTENSOR TENDON CENTRALIZATION (SAGITTAL BAND RECONSTRUCTION) PROTOCOL



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-4 (Phase I): Immobilization



Hand-based MCP extension splint: involved MCP in full extension.



IP joints free — active PIP and DIP ROM encouraged.



No active MCP flexion of involved finger.



Weeks 4-6 (Phase II): Protected Motion



Continue MCP extension splint between exercises.



Begin gentle active MCP flexion to 30-40°, progressing 10° per week.



Buddy taping to adjacent finger.



Weeks 6-8 (Phase III): Progressive Motion



Progress active MCP flexion to full range.



Wean from splint during daytime.



Continue night splinting in MCP extension until 8 weeks.



Tendon gliding exercises.



Weeks 8-12 (Phase IV): Strengthening



Discontinue splint.



Full AROM.



Progressive grip strengthening.



Functional activities.



Weeks 12+:



Full activity.



Note: Monitor for recurrent extensor tendon subluxation during MCP flexion. If subluxation recurs, return to full-time MCP extension splinting for 2 additional weeks.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Rayan GM, Murray D. Classification and treatment of closed sagittal band injuries. J Hand Surg Am, 1994; Catalano LW, Gupta S, Ragland R, Glickel SZ, Johnson C, Barron OA. Closed treatment of nonrheumatoid extensor tendon dislocations at the MCP joint. J Hand Surg Am, 2006.

RADIAL SHORTENING OSTEOTOMY PROTOCOL



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 2-3 times / week



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Weeks 0-2 (Phase I): Protection



Short arm splint or sugar-tong splint.



Active finger, thumb, elbow, and shoulder ROM.



No forearm rotation against resistance.



Weeks 2-6 (Phase II): Early Motion



Transition to removable wrist splint.



Begin gentle forearm rotation (supination/pronation).



Active wrist flexion/extension.



Gentle grip strengthening with putty.



Weeks 6-12 (Phase III): Progressive Motion



Radiographic assessment of osteotomy healing.



Progress AROM.



Progressive grip and pinch strengthening.



Functional activities.



Weeks 12+ (Phase IV): Return to Activity



Full strengthening once radiographic union confirmed (typically 8-12 weeks).



Return to sport/heavy labor at 3-4 months.



Note: Radial shortening osteotomy is less commonly performed than ulnar shortening osteotomy. Rehabilitation principles are similar. Monitor for DRUJ symptoms as relative ulnar length changes.



Modalities: Per therapist, including electrical stimulation, ultrasound, heat before, ice after.



Primary Reference: Baek GH et al. Radial shortening osteotomy for Kienböck disease. J Hand Surg Am, 2005.

CARPAL TUNNEL NERVE GLIDING EXERCISES



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 3-5 times / day



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



These exercises promote median nerve mobility within the carpal tunnel. Perform each position and hold for 5 seconds. Complete the full sequence 5-10 times per session, 3-5 times daily.



Position 1: Wrist in neutral, fingers and thumb in full flexion (fist).



Position 2: Wrist in neutral, fingers and thumb extended straight.



Position 3: Wrist and fingers extended, thumb in neutral.



Position 4: Wrist and fingers extended, thumb extended (stretched away from palm).



Position 5: Same as Position 4, forearm supinated (palm up).



Position 6: Same as Position 5, use opposite hand to gently stretch the thumb further.



Important Notes



Exercises should produce a gentle stretch — not pain.



If symptoms worsen (increased numbness, tingling, or pain), reduce the number of repetitions or range of stretch.



Combine with nighttime wrist splinting in neutral position for best results.



Ice for 10 minutes after exercises if symptoms are aggravated.



Primary Reference: Totten PA, Hunter JM. Therapeutic techniques to enhance nerve gliding in thoracic outlet syndrome and carpal tunnel syndrome. Hand Clin, 1991; Baysal O et al. Comparison of three conservative treatment protocols in carpal tunnel syndrome. Int J Clin Pract, 2006.

CUBITAL TUNNEL NERVE GLIDING EXERCISES



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 3-5 times / day



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



These exercises promote ulnar nerve mobility at the cubital tunnel. Perform each position and hold for 5 seconds. Complete the full sequence 5-10 times per session, 3-5 times daily.



Position 1: Arm at side, elbow fully flexed, wrist in neutral, fingers relaxed.



Position 2: Arm at side, elbow fully flexed, wrist extended, fingers relaxed.



Position 3: Arm at side, elbow fully flexed, wrist extended, fingers and thumb extended.



Position 4: Same as Position 3, begin to extend elbow partially (to 90°).



Position 5: Same as Position 4, extend elbow further (to 45° flexion).



Position 6: Same as Position 5, shoulder abducted to 90° with elbow extended and wrist/fingers extended.



Important Notes



Exercises should produce a gentle stretch — not pain or increased tingling.



If symptoms worsen, reduce range of elbow extension or number of repetitions.



Avoid prolonged elbow flexion past 90° during daily activities and sleep.



Consider elbow extension night splint or towel wrap to prevent sleeping in full flexion.



Ice for 10 minutes after exercises if symptoms are aggravated.



Primary Reference: Coppieters MW, Bartholomeeusen KE, Stappaerts KH. Incorporating nerve-gliding techniques in the conservative treatment of cubital tunnel syndrome. J Manipulative Physiol Ther, 2004.

FINGER STIFFNESS HOME EXERCISE PROGRAM



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: 4-6 times / day



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



Finger stiffness is common after injury, fracture, or surgery. Consistent, gentle exercises performed frequently throughout the day are more effective than aggressive, infrequent stretching.



Tendon Gliding Exercises (Perform each position, hold 5 seconds)



Straight (table-top): MCP flexed, IP joints extended.



Hook fist: MCP extended, PIP and DIP flexed.



Full fist: All joints flexed.



Straight fist: MCP and PIP flexed, DIP extended.



Full extension: All joints straight.



Perform 10 repetitions of the full sequence, 4-6 times daily.



Passive Range of Motion



Using the opposite hand, gently bend and straighten each stiff finger joint.



Hold stretches for 15-30 seconds.



Focus on the stiffest joint first.



Blocking Exercises



Stabilize one joint with the opposite hand while actively bending the next joint.



MCP blocking: Hold MCP straight, bend PIP.



PIP blocking: Hold PIP straight, bend DIP.



10 reps each, 4-6 times daily.



Edema Management



Elevate hand above heart level when resting.



Retrograde massage: Stroke from fingertip toward palm firmly, 5 minutes.



Coban wrap: Wrap finger snugly from tip to base for 10-15 minutes.



Active pumping: Open and close fist 20 times.



Important Notes



Heat before exercises (warm water soak for 10 minutes).



Ice after exercises if swelling increases.



Avoid forceful passive stretching — this can worsen stiffness and cause more swelling.



Consistency is more important than intensity.



Primary Reference: Colditz JC. Therapist's management of the stiff hand. In: Skirven TM et al., eds. Rehabilitation of the Hand and Upper Extremity, 7th ed. Elsevier, 2021.

ULNAR-SIDED WRIST PAIN HOME EXERCISE PROGRAM



\========================================



Name: ____________________________________________



Diagnosis: ____________________________________________



Date of Surgery: ____________________________________________



Frequency: Daily



Duration: 1 2 3 4 5 6



This program is for patients with ulnar-sided wrist pain (TFCC injury, ulnar impaction, ECU tendonitis) being managed non-operatively.



Phase I: Pain Control (Weeks 0-2)



Wrist brace or ulnar gutter splint for support during activities.



Ice 15-20 minutes after activity, 2-3x/day.



Avoid heavy gripping, twisting, and ulnar deviation loading.



Anti-inflammatory medication as directed.



Phase II: Gentle Motion (Weeks 2-6)



Continue brace for heavy activities.



Active wrist flexion/extension within pain-free range. 10-15 reps, 3x/day.



Gentle forearm rotation (supination/pronation). 10-15 reps, 3x/day.



Isometric wrist strengthening in neutral: press palm down, up, and sideways against table. Hold 5 sec, 10 reps each direction.



Phase III: Progressive Strengthening (Weeks 6-12)



Wrist curls (flexion and extension) with 1-2 lb weight. 2 sets of 15.



Forearm pronation/supination with hammer or weighted bar. 2 sets of 15.



Grip strengthening with putty or hand exerciser.



Dart-throwing motion exercises (combined wrist extension/radial deviation to flexion/ulnar deviation).



Gradual return to aggravating activities.



Important Notes



Avoid forceful ulnar deviation and axial loading through the wrist.



If symptoms worsen with exercises, reduce intensity and consult your surgeon.



Bracing during sports or heavy activities may be continued long-term.



Primary Reference: Atzei A, Luchetti R. Foveal TFCC tear classification and treatment. Hand Clin, 2011; Tay SC, Tomita K, Berger RA. The "ulnar fovea sign" for defining ulnar wrist pain. J Hand Surg Am, 2007.
