About Dr. Oosten

Dr. James D. Oosten is an orthopaedic surgeon specializing in conditions of the hand, wrist, forearm, elbow, and shoulder. With a foundation in bioengineering and biomechanics from the University of Pittsburgh and his medical degree from The Ohio State University College of Medicine, and his own experiences as a patient, Dr. Oosten brings a unique perspective that helps him treat your conditions in a way that is specific to you and your needs.

One thing Dr. Oosten will never take for granted is the opportunity granted to him by his childhood orthopedic surgeons. Born with severe clubfoot, he spent considerable time with orthopedic providers throughout his youth — an experience that sparked a lifelong fascination with musculoskeletal anatomy and the field of orthopedic biomechanics. Coupled with a passion for working with his hands developed through DIY projects and engineering, he became set on a career in orthopedic surgery from an early age.

His practice encompasses the full spectrum of upper extremity conditions, from acute traumatic injuries such as fractures and tendon injuries to chronic conditions including arthritis, nerve compression, and complex reconstructive cases. Dr. Oosten is committed to providing expert, compassionate surgical care to help patients restore function and return to the activities they love.

As a researcher with publications in hand and upper extremity surgery, trauma, sports medicine, spine surgery, biomechanics, and basic science, Dr. Oosten believes that a firm understanding of the science governing the procedures we do can help optimize treatment outcomes. He is also committed to using his engineering background to advance the field of Orthopaedic Surgery by improving surgical devices and items used in nonsurgical or postoperative care of patients.

What to Expect at Your Visit

A few things to know before your appointment so we can make the most of your time.

If you have had imaging done anywhere else — bring both

Bring a CD or USB drive containing the actual images, and a printed or PDF copy of the imaging report. One without the other is frequently not enough. The report tells us what a radiologist concluded; the images let us look ourselves.

For prior EMGs or previous surgeries, bring the procedure note or operative report from that surgeon, along with any post-operative instructions and any other imaging you have.

Imaging on the day

Simpler imaging such as X-rays and ultrasound can often be done the day of your appointment. Advanced imaging such as MRI and CT is usually not available same-day and generally needs to be scheduled separately, sometimes pending insurance authorization.

What to wear

For upper extremity problems we need to examine the entire arm and shoulder, and occasionally the neck. Please wear clothing that comes off easily — a loose button-down shirt is ideal. Tight sleeves make a proper exam difficult.

Costs and coverage

You may have a co-pay, and what you owe can vary depending on which services are provided at the visit. Contact your insurance provider ahead of time to understand your coverage.

If you are hoping for surgery or an injection

There may be additional treatment steps required before a procedure can be scheduled — imaging, a course of therapy, or documentation your insurer requires. The first visit is often about establishing that pathway rather than performing the procedure itself.

Disability, FMLA, and insurance paperwork

Paperwork generally cannot be completed during your appointment. Send it ahead of time or leave it with us afterwards, and allow a two-week window for it to be completed and returned.

Fax it to the office and call to let us know it is coming, so it does not sit unnoticed.

Language support

Care is provided in English and Spanish. A translator can be arranged for other languages — please request one when scheduling so it is in place before you arrive.

Post-Operative Care

Answer two questions below and this will show the guidance that applies to your stage of recovery. Your own post-operative instructions always take priority over anything shown here.

Pain Control & Medications

1

Elevation & Ice — start here, always

Keep the hand, forearm, or elbow above the level of your heart, including while sleeping — rest it on a pillow. For hand and forearm surgery, loosely tape or wrap two pillows together and nest the hand between them to stay propped up overnight. Do not place ice directly on your fingers — place it on the hand or wrist instead, which cools the fingers indirectly. Ice may go over a dressing or splint if the bag is well sealed; even when you feel no cold sensation through the splint, it still reduces swelling.

Elevate continuously · Ice 15–20 min with a cloth barrier
2

Acetaminophen (Tylenol) — scheduled

Take on a schedule for the first several days rather than waiting for pain to build. Check every other medication for hidden acetaminophen. Take less than the maximum if you have any liver disease, and speak with your primary care doctor with any questions or concerns.

No more than 4000 mg in 24 hours — less with liver disease
3

Anti-inflammatories

Layered on top of acetaminophen, not instead of it. Take with food. Appropriate only if you have no stomach or kidney disorder and have not been told you have a contraindication to anti-inflammatory medication.

With food · confirm with your surgeon after fracture or fusion surgery
4

Opioids — breakthrough only

Use only after acetaminophen and anti-inflammatories have been tried and were not enough. Opioids are prescribed by one prescriber only. If you already receive opioid medication from a pain management physician or your primary care doctor, contact that office for any additional prescriptions. Take a stool softener alongside. No driving, alcohol, or machinery.

As prescribed · shortest course possible · add a stool softener
5

Antibiotics — if prescribed

May be prescribed if you have risk factors for infection or signs of one. If prescribed, take the full course even if you are feeling better before it is finished.

Complete the entire course
6

Blood thinners — if prescribed

If a blood thinner was prescribed by another physician, continue exactly as that physician instructed. If you are prescribed aspirin after surgery, it is most likely to prevent blood clots and should be taken for the full duration recommended — after which you should resume whatever anticoagulation you were on previously.

Never stop or start anticoagulation without speaking to the prescriber

Before taking any of the above

If you have kidney disease, liver disease, are on anticoagulation, or have had a recent stroke, heart attack, or cardiac stent — or any other major medical condition — check with your surgeon or primary care physician before starting or changing any of these medications. Doses here are general guidance for healthy adults and may not be safe for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions we are asked most often after surgery.

Soft dressing: keep it dry a minimum of 24–48 hours, then you may shower and wash the incision normally with soap and water. Blot dry and apply a clean, dry bandage.

Splint or cast: it stays on and stays dry until your surgeon removes it — cover it with a trash bag sealed with Press’n Seal, or a waterproof sleeve.

Joint replacement: your dressing stays on at least one week and is normally removed by your surgeon at your post-op visit. If you cannot get to a post-op visit within the first two weeks, change it to a clean dry dressing yourself and keep the incision covered.

At one week: if your stitches are dissolvable and not visible on top of the skin, you may get the area wet and wash it lightly with soap and water. Hand incisions may be washed lightly at the one week mark.

In every case: never submerge the wound — no dishwashing, baths, pools, or hot tubs.

If you were given a sling because you had a nerve block before surgery on your elbow, forearm, wrist, or hand, you may remove it once function returns to your hand and arm and you have regained control of the limb. The sling in that situation is protecting an arm you temporarily cannot feel or move.

If you had shoulder surgery, sling use is dictated by the specific repair — please follow the post-operative instructions you were given rather than a general rule.

Not at first. Do not apply creams, lotions, salves, ointments, or powders to the wound before your first post-op visit unless specifically told to.

Cocoa butter and vitamin E may be applied once the wound has fully healed and the scabs have fallen off on their own — never pick them off.

Over-the-counter silicone cream or silicone tape may be used starting one month after surgery, or later if the scar is not yet fully healed.

If you have any questions, or if your wound healing has been delayed, check with your provider at a post-operative visit before starting any of these products.

Honey- or tan-coloured stitches are dissolvable and fall out on their own over a few weeks — leave them alone. Blue stitches or metal staples come out in the office, typically 10–14 days after surgery. If you are not sure which you have, we will tell you at your first visit.

Lifting and weight-bearing through the operated arm depend on the type of surgery you had — follow the patient handout instructions you were given.

Expect the surgical area to get sore again once you start using it. Stay ahead of that rather than chasing it: rest it, ice it, and use acetaminophen or anti-inflammatories before and after your shift, not only once it hurts.

When in doubt, describe your actual work tasks to your therapist and ask which ones are cleared. They can usually give you a far more specific answer than a general timeline.
